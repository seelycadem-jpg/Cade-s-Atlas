The Treaty of Versailles

28 June 1919·Versailles, France

The Hall of Mirrors during the signing, 28 June 1919.

France, Britain and the United States wrote the treaty to end the war with Germany and to keep Germany from attacking again. Germany signed on 28 June 1919, lost 13 percent of its European territory and every colony, and kept an army of 100,000. The treaty left Germany the largest industrial economy in central Europe and gave no power the job of enforcing the terms.

Overview

The treaty ended the war between Germany and the Allies. Germany survived as a republic. It lost 13 percent of its European territory, all its colonies and most of its army. France and Poland gained the land. The Allies set reparations at 132 billion gold marks in 1921, and Germany paid about 20 billion.

Germany had no seat at the conference that wrote the terms. Its delegation received them on 7 May 1919 and signed on 28 June under threat of invasion. Every enemy of the new republic held the signature against it.

The treaty had no means of enforcement. The US Senate rejected it, and the League of Nations had no army. By the 1930s Britain and France would not enforce the terms alone.

The Record

The accountParis, 18 January to 28 June 1919.

The conference opened in Paris on 18 January 1919. The victors met without Germany or Soviet Russia. French Prime Minister Georges Clemenceau wanted Germany too weak to attack again. British Prime Minister David Lloyd George wanted a Germany that could still trade. US President Woodrow Wilson wanted a League of Nations. Clemenceau gave up his claim to the Rhine for an Anglo-American guarantee.

The German delegation received the terms on 7 May and signed at Versailles on 28 June.

What it did, and to whomFrance and Poland gained land; Germany paid in land, arms and money.
  • Land. Germany lost 13 percent of its European territory and every colony. France got Alsace-Lorraine back, and Poland got a corridor to the sea.
  • Arms. The treaty capped the German army at 100,000 and disarmed the Rhineland.
  • Money. The Allies set reparations at 132 billion gold marks in 1921. Germany paid about 20 billion.
  • Shandong. Germany's rights in Shandong went to Japan, over China's protest.
Why it held or failedThe terms held only while Britain and France enforced them.

The treaty left Germany intact and surrounded by weak new states where Austria-Hungary and Russia had stood. The terms held only while Britain and France enforced them. By the 1930s British opinion considered German grievances fair.

One reading holds the terms were harsh enough to enrage and too weak to restrain. The other holds the terms were enforceable and the failure was the enforcers'.

The common misreadingThat reparations ruined Germany.

Germany paid about 20 billion gold marks of the 132 billion set. The hyperinflation of 1923 came from the French occupation of the Ruhr and the German government's own printing of money.

TodayThree borders the treaty drew still stand.

The Rhineland is German, Alsace is French, and the Polish corridor is Poland's coast.

The detailA vote of 11 to 6 that the chair ruled lost.

On 11 April 1919 Japan's proposal to write racial equality into the League Covenant won 11 of the 17 votes in commission. Wilson, in the chair, ruled that it needed unanimity, and the clause died.
