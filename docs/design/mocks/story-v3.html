Weimar Germany

·

The Weimar Republic lasted fourteen years, from the Kaiser's abdication in November 1918 to Hitler's appointment in January 1933. Until 1930, when chancellors began governing by presidential decree, it was the most populous democracy in Europe.

Briefing

It began at a disadvantage no other European state shared. The men who founded it had to sign the because the army told them Germany could not fight on, and from that day every enemy of the republic could say that the republic had signed Germany away. The treaty took 13 percent of the country's European land, capped its army at 100,000 and charged it reparations. It did not occupy Germany or break up its industry, so the state that resented the peace kept the means to overturn it.

The republic survived its first crisis. When Germany fell behind on reparations, France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr in January 1923; Berlin paid the strikers and printed the money, and by November the mark was worthless. A new currency and the Dawes Plan ended the emergency within a year, and American loans paid for five years of recovery. By 1928 output was above its 1913 level, Germany sat on the League Council, and the Nazis won 2.6 percent of the vote.

The did what the inflation had not. The called in the American loans; unemployment passed six million; the parties that would defend the republic lost their majority, and from 1930 chancellors governed by presidential decree. The Nazi vote rose to 37 percent by July 1932. The conservatives around President Paul von Hindenburg then appointed chancellor on 30 January 1933, expecting to use him. The treaty made the republic resented; the Depression made it unworkable; its own elite ended it.

Chapter 1 · 1919

The republic was ten weeks old when the victors met in Paris. Its delegation received the terms on 7 May 1919 and signed at Versailles on 28 June, after the army told the National Assembly it could not fight on. The right called the signature a stab in the back. The constitution followed on 11 August.

Chapter 2 · 1923

The Ruhr and the Mark

Germany fell behind on reparations in 1922, and in January 1923 French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr to take coal in kind. Berlin paid the Ruhr's workers to strike and printed the money to do it. By November a dollar bought 4.2 trillion marks. The Rentenmark stopped the fall that month, and in the same month Hitler's putsch in Munich failed in a day. Read the chapter ›

Chapter 3 · 1924 to 1929

The Dawes Plan of 1924 rescheduled reparations and opened American lending to Germany. At Locarno in 1925 Germany accepted its western frontier with France, and in 1926 it joined the League of Nations. Foreign Minister Gustav Stresemann steered both. Output passed its 1913 level. In the election of 1928 the Nazis won 2.6 percent.

Chapter 4 · 1929 to 1932

The crash of October 1929 called in the American loans that had paid for the recovery. Unemployment rose from 1.3 million in 1929 to over six million in 1932. From March 1930 Chancellor Heinrich Brüning governed by presidential decree under Article 48 and cut spending into the slump. The Nazi vote went from 2.6 percent to 18.3 in September 1930 and 37.3 in July 1932.

Chapter 5 · 1933

President Hindenburg appointed Hitler chancellor on 30 January 1933 at the urging of Franz von Papen, who expected to control him. The Reichstag fire of 27 February gave the pretext for emergency decrees, and the Enabling Act of 23 March let the cabinet make law without parliament. The republic ended under its own Article 48 and by a vote of its own Reichstag.

The Treaty of Versailles May Fourth Movement

Contributing · high confidence · 1919 → 1919

The Treaty of Versailles was one cause of May Fourth Movement among several.

The claim

The treaty's award of Germany's Shandong concessions to Japan rather than China triggered the student protests of May 4, 1919 in Beijing.

The challenge

The New Culture movement was under way from 1915; the protest's size and legacy came from that, and Shandong was the occasion.

The verdict

Contributing, high confidence. The set: the New Culture movement (ranks first), the Shandong clause (second), the warlord government's weakness (third).

The Treaty of Versailles German Hyperinflation

Contributing · high confidence · 1919 → 1923

The Treaty of Versailles was one cause of German Hyperinflation among several.

The claim

Reparations set at 132 billion gold marks in 1921 added an external debt the Weimar state could only meet by printing.

The challenge

Germany had financed the war by borrowing rather than taxing, so the mark had already lost most of its value by 1919; the 1923 collapse followed the decision to pay striking Ruhr workers with new money after the French occupation.

The verdict

Contributing, high confidence. The set: war finance (necessary), the Ruhr occupation (ranks first in 1923), the printing decision (sufficient for the final phase), reparations (third). The popular chain overstates.

The Treaty of Versailles Hitler Takes Power

Contributing · high confidence · 1919 → 1933

The Treaty of Versailles was one cause of Hitler Takes Power among several.

The claim

Versailles handed every German party a grievance that the republic, as its signatory, could not disown, and the Nazis made revision their programme.

The challenge

Weimar survived the treaty for a decade and was stabilising by 1928; the Depression collapsed the centre, and the appointment of January 1933 was the conservatives' decision.

The verdict

Contributing, high confidence, for the appointment itself. The set: the Depression (necessary), the conservatives' co-option (ranks first among the contributors), Versailles (second), Bolshevik fear (third). The treaty's weight sits on two other arrows: it made the republic's legitimacy deficit (Necessary) and decided the revisionist content of German nationalism (Necessary). The Depression decided that Germany would break; Versailles decided what the break would look like.

Stock Market Crash The Depression Reaches Germany

Necessary · high confidence · 1929 → 1930

Without Stock Market Crash, The Depression Reaches Germany does not happen.

The claim

The crash ended the short-term American lending that had financed Germany's recovery since the Dawes Plan, and the called loans carried the Depression to Germany within a year.

The challenge

German agriculture was in depression from 1927 and the economy had slowed before the crash; the credit withdrawal deepened a downturn already present.

The verdict

Necessary, high confidence. Without the withdrawal of American credit the German slowdown of 1928-29 is a recession; the 6 million unemployed of 1932 required the banking collapse that the called loans produced.

The Depression Reaches Germany Hitler Takes Power

Necessary · high confidence · 1930 → 1933

Without The Depression Reaches Germany, Hitler Takes Power does not happen.

The claim

The Depression made the Nazi party a mass movement: unemployment rose from 1.3 million in 1929 to over 6 million in 1932, and the NSDAP vote from 2.6% in 1928 to 37.3% in July 1932.

The challenge

The Nazis never won a majority and their vote fell in November 1932; Hitler was appointed by conservatives who believed they could use him. The Depression explains the vote, not the appointment.

The verdict

Necessary, high confidence. The objection is right about the mechanism of January 1933 and wrong about the grade: without a 37% party there is nothing for Papen and Hindenburg to co-opt. Co-option is Contributing; the Depression is the precondition.

German Hyperinflation Hitler Takes Power

Coincident · moderate confidence · 1923 → 1933

German Hyperinflation and Hitler Takes Power fall in the same years, with no causal link.

The claim

The hyperinflation of 1923 is popularly held to have driven Germans to Hitler.

The challenge

The NSDAP vote fell after the 1924 stabilisation to 2.6% in 1928 and rose only with deflation and unemployment in 1930-32; the memory of 1923 mattered, but the crisis that elected Hitler was deflationary.

The verdict

Coincident, moderate confidence. The inflation discredited the republic with the middle class and that mattered in 1930; as a cause of the vote it is the wrong decade.
