The options

TypefaceForCharacter
EB GaramondTitlesIn use now. A sixteenth-century book face: warm, a little antique, the closest to an old atlas.
Cormorant GaramondTitlesSharper and more delicate, with finer hairlines. Elegant large; thin and faint at card size.
Libre Caslon TextTitlesSturdier and wider, an eighteenth-century English face. Reads well small; takes more room.
NewsreaderReadingIn use now. Made for news on screens: open, even, comfortable over a long page.
Source Serif 4ReadingPlainer and more neutral. Very clear at small sizes; less personality.
LiterataReadingMade for e-books. Slightly darker and rounder; the easiest on tired eyes.
Public SansControlsIn use now. Neutral and a little wide. Clear, with no strong character.
InterControlsThe nearest thing to a standard for screens. Built for small labels and figures; very legible at 10px.
Source Sans 3ControlsNarrower and softer. Fits more in a tight bar; pairs naturally with Source Serif.
IBM Plex SansControlsMore engineered, with squarer forms. Distinct, but it pulls against the old-map feel.
SystemControlsThe phone's own face: San Francisco on an iPhone, Roboto on Android. Nothing to download, and it matches the device.

What to know before choosing

  • Is there a standard? For controls and labels, yes, in practice: Inter, or the phone's own system face. Both were drawn for small sizes on screens. For titles and reading text there is no standard; that is where a publication gets its voice.
  • Small labels are the real test. The page uses labels at 8 to 10px. A face made for screens holds up there; a book face does not. That argues for Inter or System in the Controls role.
  • Each extra typeface is a download. Three families is the usual ceiling. System costs nothing, which is why many news apps use it for controls.
  • Two serifs is unusual. Titles and reading text could share one family. The split is deliberate here: Garamond is too fine for long reading at 17px, and Newsreader is too plain for titles.
