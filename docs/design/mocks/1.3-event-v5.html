The Paris Peace Settlement

1919 to 1923·Paris·on the timeline at 28 June 1919
› · 1919 to 1923

Five treaties ended the First World War, dissolved three empires and left Germany's place in Europe unresolved.

At a glancethirty seconds

The Paris settlement dissolved three empires: Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire and Germany's overseas empire. New states replaced them, from Poland and Czechoslovakia to Iraq and Syria. survived as a republic. It lost 13 percent of its European territory and most of its army, and it was charged reparations. It remained the largest industrial economy in central Europe.

Britain, France and Japan gained the most. Britain got the two things it had fought for: the German fleet gone, and the road to India held from Palestine to Iraq, which it took from the Ottoman Empire as League mandates. France got Alsace-Lorraine, the Rhineland disarmed and Syria. Japan took Germany's northern Pacific islands and its holdings in , and with them a position across the western Pacific.

The settlement left the central question of the war unresolved: Germany's place in Europe. It also had no means of enforcement. The US Senate rejected the treaty, and the League of Nations had no army. By the 1930s Britain and France would not enforce the terms alone.

The briefthree minutes

Why it happened

Force

Germany lost the war without being broken. Its armies stood in France and Belgium at the armistice, its territory was unoccupied and its industry intact. The victors had to write a defeat that the battlefield had not made plain, against a state that remained the strongest in central Europe.

Force

France had been invaded twice in fifty years, in the of 1870 and the , and had no guaranteed ally. It wanted the Rhine as a frontier, reparations to rebuild its devastated north, and a Germany that could not come a third time.

Force

Britain wanted no rival fleet and no single power across the Channel, and it wanted the road to India held. The German navy was already interned at Scapa Flow. The Ottoman Arab provinces gave London Palestine and Iraq, astride the Suez route, which is why the mandates mattered more to Britain than any clause about Germany.

Force

Japan wanted a position across the western Pacific. Germany's islands north of the equator and its lease at Qingdao in Shandong gave it one, over China's protest and American unease.

Decision

The Council of Four chose a Germany weakened and charged but left whole. French Prime Minister gave up the Rhine for an Anglo-American guarantee that Washington never ratified; British Prime Minister kept Germany able to trade; US President took the League of Nations and lost it at home.

Trigger

The armistice of 11 November 1918 stopped the fighting before the Allies reached German soil. Many Germans did not believe they had lost. The victors met in Paris in January 1919 without Germany or Soviet Russia.

What it did, and to whom

Five treaties, one with each defeated state, redrew the map from the Rhine to the Persian Gulf. Germany lost land, colonies and its army and was charged reparations, but kept its industry. The Habsburg and Ottoman lands became new states or mandates. Britain and France gained the most ground; Japan gained the Pacific position it wanted; the United States wrote the League and then stayed out of it.

Why it held or failed

The settlement had two flaws. First, it applied self-determination selectively. Poles and Czechs got states. Germans in Austria and the Sudetenland, Hungarians, Egyptians, Koreans and Arabs did not. Every loser gained a principled case against the peace.

Second, it left Germany intact and surrounded by weak new states where Austria-Hungary and Russia had stood. The terms held only while Britain and France enforced them. By the 1930s British opinion considered German grievances fair. Hitler presented the Anschluss and the Sudeten claim as self-determination.

What it set up

Today

Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan and Israel sit largely within borders drawn under the mandates.

The detailten minutes

The account

The conference opened in Paris on 18 January 1919. Its decisions were taken by the Council of Four: Clemenceau, Lloyd George, Wilson and Italian Prime Minister Vittorio Orlando. The German delegation received the terms on 7 May and signed at Versailles on 28 June. The treaties with Austria, Bulgaria, Hungary and the Ottoman Empire followed over the next fourteen months, and Lausanne closed the settlement on 24 July 1923.

The argument

The atlas's verdictThe settlement was enforceable, and its failure was the enforcers'. Germany was weaker in 1919 than at any point since 1871, and the terms held for as long as Britain and France were willing to act.
The strongest case againstThe terms were harsh enough to enrage and too weak to restrain: they gave every German party a grievance while leaving Germany the largest economy in central Europe. On this reading the failure was built into the text.

The common misreading

That reparations ruined Germany. Germany paid about 20 billion gold marks of the 132 billion set, and its economy outgrew France's across the 1920s. The hyperinflation of 1923 followed the Ruhr occupation and the government's own printing, which is its own page.

The detail

On 11 April 1919 Japan's proposal to write racial equality into the League Covenant won 11 of the 17 votes in commission. Wilson, in the chair, ruled that it needed unanimity, and the clause died.
