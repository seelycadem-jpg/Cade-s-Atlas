The Ruhr and the Mark

1922 to 1923·the Ruhr and Berlin·eight events

Berlin paid for resistance in the Ruhr with printed money, and the mark lost all its value.

Germany lost its currency in 1923 because Berlin paid for resistance in the Ruhr with printed money. In January a dollar bought about 18,000 marks; in November it bought 4.2 trillion. Savings, pensions and war bonds held in marks lost all their value.

The fall began before the Ruhr. Germany paid for the war by borrowing and printing rather than by taxing. By the end of 1919 a dollar bought about 47 marks, against 4.2 in 1914. The London Schedule of May 1921 then set reparations at 132 billion gold marks. Berlin covered its deficits at the printing press, and the added a foreign debt it could only meet the same way.

Germany fell behind on deliveries of timber and coal, and the Reparations Commission declared it in default. On 11 January 1923 French and Belgian troops entered the to take the coal in kind, because French Prime Minister wanted the payments enforced. Chancellor answered with passive resistance and paid the idle miners and railwaymen from the press. Output stopped in the country's industrial heart while the wage bill grew.

Five days later Berlin fixed the old money at 4.2 trillion to the dollar: the pre-war rate with twelve zeros added. The printing stopped, and the new currency held.

The year shook the republic from both sides. Communists rose in Hamburg in October, and Adolf Hitler and General Erich Ludendorff tried to seize Bavaria on 8 and 9 November. Both risings failed within days. The putsch made Hitler known across Germany, but the inflation did not make him a power: in 1928, with the mark stable, the Nazis won 2.6 percent of the vote. The Dawes Plan of 1924 rescheduled reparations, and France left the Ruhr by August 1925.

The constitution

The National Assembly met at Weimar from February 1919 because Berlin was unsafe, and adopted the constitution on 11 August 1919. It gave Germany universal suffrage, proportional representation and a directly elected president who could rule by decree under Article 48. Every chancellor from 1930 governed under that article.

The common misreading

That the hyperinflation of 1923 drove Germans to Hitler. Five years later, with the mark stable, the Nazis won 2.6 percent. The vote that made them a mass party came after 1929, with the Depression. The atlas grades the link from the hyperinflation to Hitler as coincident: same country, same decade, no line.
