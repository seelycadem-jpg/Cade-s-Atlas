Four ways to mark a knot, a fibre and a strand

The same three words everywhere, and one mark for each that reads at 14 pixels on a phone, beside a title, on the timeline and on the globe, in light and in dark. Each set is shown in all four places. The thread rule holds for every set: filled or heavier means bigger.

AWeight

What the mocks use today. Filled, ring, dot: the eye reads size as ink. Plainest of the four, and the one most like a timeline convention readers already know.

BThe map legend

The settlement hierarchy every old atlas prints in its key: a ringed dot for the capital, a filled dot for a city, a small ring for a town. Readers have seen it since school, and it says the same thing the atlas means: this one matters more. Carries the atlas feel without costume.

CThe thread

Literal: a knot tied in the line, a bead on the line, a tick on the line. Strongest on the chain view, where the thread is drawn anyway. Weakest beside a title and on the globe, where the line through each mark has nothing to join.

DShapes

Diamond, square, dot: three shapes, so size is told by form as well as weight and survives colour-blindness and a bad screen. Old maps mark battles with crossed swords and forts with squares; this borrows the habit. Loudest of the four.

My recommendation: B, the map legend.

It is the only set that is already a convention for exactly this meaning, bigger place and bigger mark, and it belongs to the atlas world by right rather than by decoration. A reads well but says nothing; C breaks outside the chain; D shouts. B's one risk is the knot mark at 12 pixels on a dense timeline, where the inner dot can close up. The timeline strip under B shows it at that size, and it holds.
