# Meanwhile Atlas: story methodology and anti-slop rules (internal)

Companion to claude/house-style.md. Full version with diagrams in the "Meanwhile Atlas: Analytic Standards" doc. Rule zero (no negation-then-reveal) and rule one (brief register everywhere; Cicero in the themes layer only) apply throughout.

## New structure for the fresh build (agreed in discussion 2026-11-02; supersedes the vocabulary below once built)

Mission (Analytic Standards doc, first paragraph): Meanwhile Atlas is for the curious, people who want to know how humanity's story fits together. Four pillars: narrative (the chain of events), the global and country view, the themes, the timeline.

Levels, each made of the summaries of the level below, each complete writing:
- Chronicle: the long story of a civilisation, empire or country ("The Ottomans, 1299 to 1922"). Its opening is a descriptive, analytic portrait of what it was historically (what kind of power, what it held, its place in the world, how it ended). It carries no thesis. Its page = its story summaries; reading only those gives the rise and fall in about two minutes. "Chronicle" was on the rejected list for a generic container; it is now kept for this place-bound meaning ("Saga" considered and set aside: it suggests legend).
- Story: a major phase of a chronicle (Rise, Height, Stalemate, Retreat and Reform, Fall). The story carries the argument and the context: the vacuum or forces it grew from, why it went as it did, its significance (e.g. Rise opens with the Fourth Crusade, the Mongol breaking of the Seljuks and the fall of Baghdad). A story that spans places belongs to two or more chronicles and shows in each.
- Patch: one narrative paragraph inside a story, built around exactly one knot.
- Knot, strand, fibre: three sizes of event, all events with a page and at least one arrow (revised 2026-11-03, see narrative-flow.md). Knots are the turning points; strands the steps between knots; fibres the lesser-known events for the deep reader. Detail (figures, documents, quotes) is a section of a page, never a tier. Tier belongs to the pair of event and chain. Going deeper opens the event as its own chain; there is no fourth tier. Readers meet these as "go deeper" with plain labels (turning points / the full story / every detail); the textile words stay internal.
- Worked example: the Ottoman chronicle in the Analytic Standards doc (chronicle overview, five story summaries, Rise opened into five patches on Bapheus 1302, Gallipoli 1354, Kosovo 1389, Ankara 1402, Constantinople 1453). Written from memory: verify before it ships.
- Open: whether cross-place stories need their own name (thread was suggested); whether the six arcs drafted on 2026-11-01 (review/events_anc/arcs) are recut into chronicles and stories.

## Vocabulary shown to readers (agreed 2026-10-28)

Sizes: Arc › Story › Thread. Tiers inside a story: Knots (turning points, the events the chain cannot do without) · Strands (the events a story is made of, with their arrows) · Fibres (the fine detail). The tier switch in every story reads Knots · Strands · Fibres with a plain subtitle under each (the turning points · the events · the details). "Arc" stays plain: a weave has no beginning or end to follow; the textile words describe how a story is made, never what the whole is. If after use the subtitles are doing all the work, revert to the plain words. Internally the tier matrix and the words Tier 1/2/3 are unchanged.

Rejected names, so they are not proposed again: Strategic/Operational/Tactical; Strategic/Analytic/Ground; Gist/Argument/Evidence; Skim/Read/Study; Pattern/Mechanism/Particular; Structure/Conjuncture/Event; Horizon/Terrain/Ground; Landmarks/Milestones; Hinges/Steps; Chronicle (as a generic container; see above); Case; Weave (for arc).

## Places in the UI

Atlas (the globe, the year, the doors) › Arc › Story (with the Knots/Strands/Fibres switch) › Event. The timeline and globe follow whatever is being read. A breadcrumb always says where the reader is. The old dashboard layout is retired; the shell is being rebuilt clean on this model.

## Sizes: Arc, Story, Thread

- Arc: world-spanning, each with a thesis. "The German Question".
- Story: the default unit, three to seven chapters. "The post-Soviet struggles".
- Thread: a story bounded to one place or one conflict, two to four chapters, written the same way. "Transnistria".
Size is a label set by scope (five tests: span, reach, fan-out, load, thesis level). Nesting is as deep as the links go; the reader sees a tree drawn from whichever parent they came through, and a story page lists every parent with its grade and date. Storage is flat links, never a nesting field. Each level is a real story with a thesis and hinges, never a folder.

## Four phases, three loops

1. DEFINE: name the contest (a tension, never a period) → thesis (one sentence that could be wrong) → assumptions (3 at most) + disconfirmation line → read the shelf (3-6 real works; find the awkward fact).
2. SELECT: list candidates → score tiers (reach, irreversibility, load) → pick hinges (3-7 chapters, one hinge each) → connector test (therefore or but; an and-then means merge or cut the chapter).
3. CONNECT: grade each arrow (type, grade, confidence) → write notes (claim, objection, warrant, verdict) → link theatres (story-to-story, graded) → cross-region check (every T1 hinge leaves its region).
4. WRITE AND CHECK: write the argument level first, compress to the knots, assemble the fibres → slop check (fails: rewrite) → red team ("where would an expert stop reading") (fails: regrade) → editor signs → published with audit line.

## Naming

Name the contest or question, never the period or place. Arc titles are the thesis compressed into two to four plain words ("Unity Without a State", "The Insecurity of Size"), or a term of art the field uses; no puns, no borrowed lines, no topic labels; the subtitle carries the scope. Theatres are not named; they carry the parent's name on the link.

## Five parts of a story

Thesis · Assumptions + disconfirmation · Chapters (3-7 beats, one hinge each, unequal length, connector never in the prose) · Theatres · Where it reaches today (checkable, written forward, no flourish).

## Reading a story (the tier switch)

Knots: thesis, then one sentence per knot with its connector badge, then the today paragraph; 90 seconds. Strands: every event in the chain with a short paragraph and its graded arrow; contested arrows show both schools; 10 minutes. Fibres: everything, with figures, evidence tests and audit lines. Each is complete writing, never a truncation. Tapping any event at any tier opens the event.

## Themes (built at stage 5, after two chains are linked)

Ten themes in the Cicero register: Man and Power, Man and God, Man and War, Man and Scarcity, Man and Machine, Man and Nature, Man and the Stranger, Man and Truth, Man and Time, Man and Memory. Each is a tension between two poles with cases at both; each holds propositions in the brief register and shows the evolution of thought on its question as a story in the ideas lane, with the events lane beside it. No bare names: every thinker, work or event named is a node. Every theme's shelf draws on at least three civilisations. Ideological links need evidence the actors read or invoked the idea.

## Against slop (hard-check and red-team rules)

- Rule zero: no negation-then-reveal, in any register.
- Expert's nod: every chapter has one fact a general reader wouldn't know and an expert would recognise.
- Awkward fact: every story includes one thing that doesn't fit the thesis, stated as such.
- Name the shelf: 3-6 real works by author and title per story; every Contested arrow names its schools.
- Specific verbs, specific actors: institutions and people rather than capitals.
- Disconfirmation line: what would have to be true for the thesis to be wrong.
- Provenance: basis on every figure, shelf on every story, audit line on every T1 link. Every event keeps its Wikipedia and Britannica exits.
- No invented specifics: a name, date or figure that can't be sourced is left out.
- Banned moves: aphoristic closers and antitheses; "existential", "shaped", "in many ways", "complex interplay", "the stage was set", "it is no coincidence", "in other words", "it is important to note", "tapestry"; triplets for rhythm; rhetorical questions outside the themes layer; "for the first time in N centuries" unless sourced; equal-length beats; connector words in prose; hedging boilerplate; a today paragraph ending on a flourish.
- Red-team prompt: "where would you stop reading, and why".
