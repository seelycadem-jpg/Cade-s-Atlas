  now: The Caprivi Strip's odd shape on Namibia's map comes from this treaty.
  story: Britain got Zanzibar as a protectorate and Germany dropped claims in Uganda and Witu. Germany got the island of Heligoland and the Caprivi Strip in Namibia.
  why: Britain wanted control of the Nile's sources and Zanzibar; Germany wanted Heligoland to protect its naval coast.
  then: Britain's grip on Zanzibar led to the 1896 bombardment, and Germany turned Heligoland into a naval fortress.
## af-lugalo | 1891 | Mkwawa's Hehe destroy a German column at Lugalo | who: Chief Mkwawa's Hehe army vs. Emil von Zelewski's Schutztruppe | where: Lugalo, near Iringa, Tanzania
  bluf: The Hehe ambush at Lugalo was the heaviest early defeat suffered by colonial forces in German East Africa. It showed African states could fight effectively, though Germany won in the end through a brutal, years-long campaign.
  now: The defeat forced Germany to fight a seven-year war against the Hehe and to garrison the interior, which made German East Africa a military colony; Mkwawa's resistance became a founding story of Tanzanian nationalism.
  story: The Hehe were the strongest power in the southern highlands and controlled caravan routes. On August 17, 1891, about 3,000 Hehe warriors ambushed Zelewski's force of about 320 askari plus officers and porters. Zelewski was killed.
  why: Germany wanted to break Hehe control of the trade routes; Mkwawa refused to submit.
  then: Germans stormed Mkwawa's fortress at Kalenga in 1894; he fought a guerrilla war until 1898.
  fact: Lugalo is celebrated in Tanzania as a symbol of resistance.
## af-uganda | 1894 | Britain makes Uganda a protectorate | who: British government | where: Kampala
  bluf: Britain declared a protectorate over Uganda mainly to control the source of the Nile and so protect its position in Egypt. Strategic worries about water drove the colonization of a whole region.
  now: The Nile's waters still shape regional politics, including disputes over Ethiopia's giant dam.
  story: The Imperial British East Africa Company could not afford to hold Uganda. Fearing another power would control the source of the Nile, and so Egypt's water, the British government declared a protectorate in 1894.
  why: To control the source of the Nile and protect Britain's position in Egypt.
  then: Britain built a railway from Mombasa to Lake Victoria to hold it, and ruled largely through the Buganda kingdom's chiefs.
## af-uganda-b | 1895 | East Africa Protectorate (future Kenya) | who: British government | where: Mombasa
  bluf: Britain's takeover of the company's coastal territory created the future Kenya, a colony shaped by a railway and by white settlement in the fertile highlands. Land taken for settlers became the central grievance of Kenyan nationalism.
  now: Land taken for settlers became a central grievance behind the Mau Mau uprising.
  story: In July 1895 Britain took over the company's territory between the coast and Uganda as the East Africa Protectorate, with its capital at Mombasa. The capital moved to Nairobi in 1905, and the territory became Kenya Colony in 1920.
  why: Britain wanted to secure the route between the coast and Uganda, and so the headwaters of the Nile.
  then: The railway and white settlers in the highlands followed, and Africans were pushed into reserves.
## af-railway | 1896 | Uganda Railway: the 'Lunatic Line' | who: British government; over 30,000 Indian laborers | where: Mombasa to Kisumu, Kenya
  bluf: The Uganda Railway linked the coast to Lake Victoria, securing British control of the Nile's headwaters and making the colony pay. It shaped Kenya's geography: Nairobi, white settlement in the highlands and the Indian community all grew from it.
  now: Nairobi exists because of the railway; Kenya's Indian community descends partly from its workers.
  story: Begun in 1896 at Mombasa, it reached Lake Victoria in 1901. Over 30,000 laborers came from British India; several thousand died. In 1898 two lions at Tsavo killed at least 28 workers. Nairobi grew from a railway camp.
  why: Britain wanted to secure Uganda and the Nile, end the inland slave caravans and make the colony pay for itself.
  then: White settlers took fertile highlands along the line, and Indian traders and workers settled in towns along its route.
  myth: Critics in Parliament mocked it as 'the Lunatic Line' because it seemed to go from nowhere to nowhere.
## af189627 | 1896 | Ethiopia defeats Italy at Adwa | who: Emperor Menelik II, Empress Taytu | where: Adwa, Ethiopia
  bluf: Ethiopia's crushing defeat of Italy at Adwa preserved its independence while almost all of Africa was being colonized. It proved an African state could beat a modern European army, and became a lasting symbol of African dignity and pan-Africanism.
  now: Adwa inspired pan-Africanism, Ethiopia's colours appear on many African flags, and Addis Ababa is home to the African Union.
  story: On March 1, 1896, Emperor Menelik II's army of around 100,000 men, armed with modern rifles, destroyed an Italian invasion force at Adwa. About 7,000 Italian soldiers were killed. Italy recognised Ethiopia's independence that October.
  why: Italy claimed a protectorate over Ethiopia under a clause of the 1889 Treaty of Wuchale whose Amharic and Italian versions differed. Menelik had united Ethiopia's regional lords and built a large army with modern rifles, many bought from France and Russia.
  then: Ethiopia was the only African state to defeat a European power and stay free, apart from Liberia. Italy invaded again under Mussolini in 1935, seeking revenge.
  fact: Ethiopia's green, yellow and red were later adopted by many newly independent African states as pan-African colors.
## af189628 | 1896 | Anglo-Zanzibar War | who: Britain vs. Sultan Khalid bin Barghash | where: Zanzibar
  bluf: The 40-minute bombardment of Zanzibar's palace showed what a British 'protectorate' meant: Britain decided who could rule. It ended any independent authority of the sultanate.
  now: The bombardment installed a sultan who did as Britain asked, ended Zanzibar's independence and abolished its slave market; British control of the coast and the Swahili interior followed.
  story: When Sultan Hamad died on August 25, 1896, his cousin Khalid seized the palace without British approval. On August 27 British warships shelled the palace for about 40 minutes until Khalid's flag came down; about 500 of his defenders were killed or wounded.
  why: Khalid seized the palace without British approval, and Britain, which claimed the right to approve the sultan, chose to make an example.
  then: A pro-British sultan was installed, and Zanzibar remained a British protectorate until 1963.
  fact: At about forty minutes, it is the shortest recorded war.
## af-zanzslavery | 1897 | Zanzibar abolishes slavery | who: Sultan of Zanzibar under British pressure | where: Zanzibar
  bluf: Zanzibar abolished slavery 24 years after closing the market, and many freed people stayed on plantations as poorly paid workers. Inequalities between Arab landowners and African laborers fed the violent revolution of 1964.
  now: Zanzibar's 1964 revolution, which overthrew the Arab-dominated government, was rooted partly in the inequalities left by slavery.
  story: In April 1897 the Sultan, under British pressure, abolished the legal status of slavery on Zanzibar and Pemba. It was a cautious decree: enslaved people had to apply to a court for freedom, owners were compensated, and concubines were excluded until 1909. Relatively few applied at first, because freedom brought no land or income, and many stayed on the clove plantations as low-paid workers or squatters.
  why: British pressure after the 1896 bombardment, and Britain's need to justify its protectorate as an anti-slavery mission.
  then: Slavery on the mainland lingered for years: Kenya's coast ended it only in 1907, and German East Africa never fully abolished it.
## af-mkwawa | 1898 | Mkwawa dies rather than surrender | who: Chief Mkwawa | where: Mlambalasi, near Iringa
  bluf: Mkwawa's death ended the Hehe resistance, one of the longest against German rule. The return of his skull from Germany in 1954 became an early symbol of colonial restitution.
  now: Mkwawa is a national hero in Tanzania, and the return of his skull is an early landmark in debates over restitution of colonial-era remains and objects.
  story: Mkwawa had fought on since his fortress at Kalenga fell in 1894. Surrounded, he took his own life on July 19, 1898. Germans cut off his head as a trophy.
  why: German forces had hunted Mkwawa for four years after his fortress fell.
  then: His skull was taken to Germany; it was returned in 1954 and is now in a memorial museum at Kalenga.
  myth: The Treaty of Versailles (1919) actually ordered Germany to hand over 'the skull of the Sultan Mkwawa'. It was finally returned in 1954.
## af-omdurman | 1898 | Battle of Omdurman | who: Kitchener's British-Egyptian army vs. the Mahdist state | where: Omdurman, Sudan
  bluf: Omdurman showed the brutal technology gap behind colonial conquest: machine guns killed about 10,000 Sudanese in a morning for a few dozen British dead. It destroyed the Mahdist state and gave Britain control of the Nile valley.
  now: It is a stark example of the technology gap behind colonial conquest, and Sudan's divisions between north and south deepened under the Anglo-Egyptian rule that followed.
  story: Kitchener's army, with Maxim guns and artillery, faced about 50,000 Mahdist troops outside Omdurman on September 2, 1898. About 10,000 Sudanese were killed; fewer than 50 British troops died.
  why: Britain wanted to control the Nile valley and avenge General Gordon's death at Khartoum in 1885.
  then: Britain and Egypt ruled Sudan jointly as the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan until 1956.
## af-omdurman-b | 1898 | Fashoda Incident | who: Kitchener; French Major Jean-Baptiste Marchand | where: Fashoda (Kodok), South Sudan
  bluf: The Fashoda standoff brought Britain and France close to war over the Upper Nile. France's retreat led the two to settle their colonial disputes and align against Germany in the Entente Cordiale.
  now: Fashoda settled that France would leave the Nile to Britain, which cleared the way for the Entente Cordiale of 1904 and the alliance that fought Germany ten years later.
  story: A small French expedition had crossed Africa to plant the flag at Fashoda on the White Nile. Kitchener arrived with a much larger force after Omdurman. After weeks of tension, France, with the weaker navy, backed down.
  why: France wanted a belt of territory across Africa from west to east; Britain wanted the whole Nile from Cairo to Uganda.
  then: The two rivals settled their colonial disputes in the Entente Cordiale of 1904.
  fact: 'Fashoda syndrome' is still used for French wariness of British influence in Africa.
## af189929 | 1899 | Boer War | who: Britain vs. Boer republics (Transvaal, Orange Free State) | where: South Africa
  bluf: Britain's war against the Boer republics secured South Africa's gold for the empire at terrible cost, including concentration camps where about 26,000 Boer civilians, most of them children, and at least 14,000 Black Africans died. The peace that followed reconciled whites at the expense of Black South Africans.
  now: The camps and scorched-earth tactics foreshadowed 20th-century warfare against civilians, and Afrikaner bitterness fed the nationalism behind apartheid.
  story: Britain wanted the Transvaal's gold fields. Boer guerrillas fought on; Britain burned farms and held about 100,000 civilians in camps where tens of thousands died.
  why: Control of the Witwatersrand gold fields, British demands for rights for foreign miners in the Transvaal, and imperial ambitions to unite southern Africa under British rule.
  then: Britain won in 1902, and in 1910 created the Union of South Africa, a white-ruled dominion that excluded Black Africans from power.
## d-goldenstool | 1900 | War of the Golden Stool | who: Queen Mother Yaa Asantewaa; British colonial forces | where: Kumasi, Ghana
  bluf: The British demand to sit on the Golden Stool, Asante's sacred symbol, set off the last great Asante revolt. Britain annexed Asante, but the Stool was never surrendered, a sign that conquest could not capture a people's sense of sovereignty.
  now: Yaa Asantewaa is a national heroine in Ghana and a symbol of women's leadership in anti-colonial resistance.
  story: In March 1900 Governor Frederick Hodgson demanded the Golden Stool, the symbol of the Asante nation. Led by Yaa Asantewaa, Asante fighters besieged the British fort at Kumasi for months before a relief force broke the revolt.
  why: Britain had exiled the Asante king in 1896 and wanted complete control.
  then: Asante was annexed to the Gold Coast colony in 1901, but the British never got the Stool.
## af190430 | 1904 | Herero and Nama genocide | who: German colonial army under Gen. Lothar von Trotha | where: German South West Africa (Namibia)
  bluf: Germany's campaign against the Herero and Nama in Namibia is widely recognized as the first genocide of the 20th century. Its methods, from an extermination order to concentration camps and racial science, foreshadowed later atrocities.
  now: Germany acknowledged it as genocide in 2021 and offered development aid, but many Herero and Nama groups rejected the deal as inadequate.
  story: After the Herero and Nama rose against land seizures, General von Trotha ordered their extermination. Survivors were driven into the desert or concentration camps.
  why: German settlers seized Herero and Nama land and cattle, and abuse and debt schemes drove the Herero to revolt in 1904, followed by the Nama.
  then: Up to 80% of the Herero and about half of the Nama died. Survivors were held in camps, and their land and cattle were confiscated.
  fact: Germany formally acknowledged it as genocide in 2021.
