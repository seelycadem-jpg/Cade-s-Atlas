# Event page: structure, principles and review (Meanwhile Atlas)

## What an event page is
Meanwhile Atlas is a world-history atlas for curious, intelligent readers. Every event page explains what the event did to the order of the world and why. The model is a senior foreign-policy analyst writing for a principal: Richard Haass (Council on Foreign Relations; "The World", "A World in Disarray") and Ian Bremmer (Eurasia Group, GZERO). Judgment first, system-level, winners and losers, why it worked or failed, what it set up. Pithy. Hard facts, no scenery.

**Scope rule.** When an event is the centre of something larger (a peace, a war, a revolution), the page covers the whole thing. Its parts are strands listed at the bottom (e.g. the Paris Peace Settlement page covers Versailles, Saint-Germain, Trianon, Neuilly, Sèvres; each of those is a strand with its own short page).

## Structure (fixed; headings shown to readers in this order)
1. **Title, dates.**
2. **Overview.** Written like a presidential daily brief: short declarative sentences, one fact or judgment each, none over 25 words, in two or three short paragraphs, about 150 words. It gives the so-what in hard facts. What it ended, who won and who lost (with what they gained or lost), what it left unsolved, why it held or failed. First sentence is the judgment. No abstractions ("the order", "a principle"). A reader who stops here has the event. Model: the owner's own draft for Paris was "it ended three major empires, brought Germany to its knees, did not solve fundamental questions, greatly rewarded the UK, France and Japan with more territorial possessions, and lacked enforcement capabilities"; the overview extends that with the facts under each point.
3. **Why it happened.** 2-3 sentences. The forces (structural) and the decision (who chose, what they wanted). Name actors with role.
4. **What it did.** The terms or actions as hard facts, with numbers. Bullets allowed, at most five, each one line.
5. **Winners and losers.** Who gained, who lost, who paid. Short declaratives.
6. **Why it held or failed.** The analytic core: 3-4 sentences on the mechanism and the misjudgment. This is the lesson a reader carries to a situation the atlas never wrote.
7. **What it set up.** 2-4 consequences, each one line, each the name of a later event the page links to.
8. **Today.** One checkable line: an institution, border, grievance or practice still live.
9. **The detail.** One expert's-nod fact a general reader would not know and an expert would recognise.
10. **Strands.** The component events, as a list of names with years.

Target length for 2-8: 350-450 words for a single event, up to 600 for a knot spanning several strands.

## Principles
- Overview first: the so-what in hard facts. The first sentence is a judgment, never a description or scene.
- Write about the system: who held power before and after, which rules changed.
- Name winners, losers and who paid.
- A fact earns its place only if it supports the judgment. No colour, no atmosphere, no anniversaries for effect.
- Say why it held or failed: what the decision-makers misjudged or got right.
- One judgment per paragraph, carried by its first sentence. Paragraphs at most four sentences.
- Probabilities, not prophecy: "made more likely", "raised the cost of"; never "inevitable", "doomed".
- Plain, confident, declarative. Active voice, strong verbs.

## Banned (any one sends the draft back)
- Storytelling openings, scene-setting, "On a June morning...", "five years to the day".
- Negation-then-reveal: "It did not X. It Y.", "not X but Y", "The question is not X but Y". A contrast goes in one clause with "rather than" only when the contrast is the point.
- Rule-of-three lists and stacked constructions built for rhythm; "not only X, but also Y".
- Stock transitions: "In conclusion", "It's important to note", "In today's world", "A testament to".
- Words: shaped, pivotal, tapestry, delve, leverage, navigate, robust, existential, underscore, paved the way, ushered in, the stage was set, in many ways, complex interplay, it is no coincidence.
- Idioms, cliches and figurative phrases ("finished off", "took the spoils", "made the terms stick", "humiliated rather than crippled"); use the plain literal verb.
- Aphoristic closers; rhetorical questions; symmetrical sentence lengths; filler adverbs.
- Naming historians or books in the text.
- Any name, number or date that cannot be sourced.

## Review (three independent reviewers, then a merge)
- **Fact-checker.** Verifies every factual claim (names, dates, numbers, terms) against Wikipedia, Britannica or a university or museum source. Returns a table: claim · verdict (correct / wrong / imprecise / unsupported) · correction · source URL.
- **Style editor (the Haass/Bremmer desk).** Checks the draft against the structure, principles and banned list. Returns: the violations by line, a tightened line edit of every section, and a score out of 10 for "would this run in Foreign Affairs or a Eurasia Group note".
- **Red team (the analyst).** Attacks the judgments, not the prose. Is the bottom line right? What is the strongest case against it (the scholarly counter-reading)? Is anything important missing from winners and losers or why it failed? Returns: the counter-case, what the draft should concede or sharpen, and a better bottom line if one exists.
- **Merge.** The editor in chief takes the fact corrections as binding, adopts line edits that obey the rules, decides each red-team point (adopt, concede in one clause, or reject with a reason), and publishes.

## Linking
Every event the text names that the atlas holds is linked: the words that name it (or the phrase that describes it) carry the link, never a bare date. Example: in "the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople in 1204", the words "sacked Constantinople" link to the Sack of Constantinople (1204). In storage write the link inline as [[event-id|words]] so it survives renaming and the build can list every page that mentions an event. An event with no page yet is listed in a "## To write" section at the end of the draft and stays plain text. A link is navigation, not a causal claim; graded arrows live in the chain, not in the prose.

## Visuals
Propose one or two small visuals only where they show what the prose cannot; none if they would only decorate. Kinds: a before-and-after map (wars, treaties, partitions, conquests: borders the year before and after, changed territory marked; drawn from the atlas border data, maps/own.json); a chart for a number that moves (demanded vs paid, population before and after); a short timeline for a sequence of crises; a diagram of who stood with whom. In the draft, add a "## Visuals" section: for each, the kind, the years or data, the caption (one line, what it shows) and the sources for any numbers. Visual numbers are fact-checked like the text.
