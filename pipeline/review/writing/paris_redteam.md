# Red team: The Paris Peace Settlement, 1919–1923

## Verdict on the bottom line
Half right. The enforcement point is the draft's best insight and should stay. Three things are wrong with how it is framed.

1. **"Harsh enough to breed revision" fights the draft's own evidence and the modern consensus.** The draft says Germany kept 87% of its European territory and paid 20.6 billion of a 50-billion real bill. The mainstream scholarly view now is that the terms were a defensible compromise, not unusually harsh by the standard of 1871 or Brest-Litovsk. On that view the revisionism came from German refusal to accept that Germany had lost the war, not from the severity of the terms. The opposite view, that the terms were too lenient, is also live: Germany was left intact, with its ambitions unchecked. Neither school would endorse "harsh" without qualification.
2. **The Senate vote carries more causal weight than it can bear.** A vote in March 1920 does not explain why Britain and France, who between them still held overwhelming military superiority, did nothing in March 1936. Britain made its own guarantee conditional on American ratification, then guaranteed only the western borders at Locarno (1925). The enforcement failure was a series of British and French choices made in the 1930s, under the Depression. The Senate's refusal was the first of those defections, and not the decisive one.
3. **Sentence 2 is a Germany-only chronology rather than a consequence.** It drops the Middle East, which sentence 1 raises, and stops short of 1939.

## The strongest counter-case (3-4 sentences)
The settlement failed on legitimacy before it failed on enforcement. It rested on self-determination, then applied the principle selectively. Austrians were barred from union with Germany. Three million Germans were placed in Czechoslovakia and 3.3 million Hungarians abroad. Egypt, Korea, China and the Arab provinces were denied the principle altogether, so the defeated and the colonised had a principled case against the peace from the day it was signed. That case eroded the will to enforce at its source. British opinion came to see the German grievances as fair, which is why Rhineland, Anschluss and Sudetenland were each defended as self-determination and met no resistance. A peace that the victors' own publics thought unjust was never going to be enforced, with or without the Senate.

Supporting readings, in descending order of weight:
- **Moderate terms, failure in the 1930s.** The modern consensus holds that the terms were payable and survivable. The failure came from German rejection of defeat, the Depression, and British and French decisions not to act.
- **Strategic, not punitive.** The settlement left a united Germany facing small, mutually hostile successor states where Austria-Hungary and Russia had stood. That arguably improved Germany's relative position in central Europe compared with 1914.
- **Global.** For most of the world the settlement's lasting work was outside Germany. It extended empire through the mandates. It disappointed the anticolonial movements that had invoked Wilson in 1919 (the "Wilsonian moment"). It drew the Middle East's state system. And Turkey showed within four years that a treaty (Sèvres) could be overturned by force.

## Section by section: concede or sharpen

**Bottom line.** Drop "harsh". Move the point of failure from 1920 to the 1930s. Bring in the Middle East and colonial side, or cut the Middle East from sentence 1 so the page does not promise what it never delivers. See the proposed text below.

**Why it happened.** Add one clause on the strategic decision. The war ended in an armistice before any invasion of Germany, so the victors were dictating to a state that had not been occupied and did not believe it had lost. That decision is the root of the legitimacy problem, and it belongs here. Also name Lloyd George, the British Prime Minister. Britain is absent from the whole page, even though it was the largest imperial gainer and the power whose defection decided the 1930s.

**What it did.** The bullets are sound. Consider replacing the Lausanne bullet, which is a procedural fact, with the minorities treaties: the new and enlarged states signed minority protection obligations under League supervision. That records the self-determination mechanism the counter-case turns on.

**Winners and losers.** This is the weakest section. It reads as a list of territorial transfers, without a judgment about who held power afterwards. Missing:
- **Winners:** Britain and France as empires, whose holdings grew through the mandates; the new and enlarged states (Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Greater Romania), which were the settlement's main beneficiaries and its weak point in the east; Turkey, the one defeated power that revised its treaty by arms and won.
- **Losers:** Italy, a victor that came away denied Fiume and Dalmatia (the "mutilated victory"), which fed the March on Rome in 1922; China, whose Shandong loss is mentioned only as Japan's gain; the Arab national movement, whose Syrian kingdom France crushed at Maysalun in 1920; Soviet Russia, excluded, which turned to Germany at Rapallo in 1922, one excluded power dealing with the other.
- **Who paid:** keep the population exchange. Add the minorities left on the wrong side of the new borders as the settlement's standing cost.
- **Proposed rewrite (about 60 words):** "Britain and France emerged as the leading imperial powers, holding the Arab mandates. Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia and Romania gained most in Europe. Italy, a victor, was refused Fiume and called it a mutilated victory. China lost Shandong to Japan. Hungary lost proportionally most, leaving 3.3 million Hungarians abroad. Some 1.6 million Greek Orthodox and Muslims paid in Lausanne's compulsory exchange."

**Why it held or failed.** The mechanism is right but too narrow, and sentences 1 and 4 say the same thing. Concede three points:
- the 87% figure cuts both ways: it supports "needed enforcement", and it is also evidence that the terms were not crippling;
- Britain's conditional guarantee and its east-west split at Locarno (1925) were British choices;
- the decisive non-enforcement came in 1936–38, when Britain and France had the means.

Sharpen the misjudgment. The peacemakers assumed that a principle (self-determination) and an institution (the League) could substitute for a standing commitment by the great powers. The principle then supplied Germany's justification for each step.
- **Proposed rewrite (4 sentences):** "The settlement needed great powers willing to enforce terms that the defeated never accepted. The US Senate's 49–35 vote in March 1920 fell short of two-thirds, and the Anglo-American guarantee to France lapsed with it. Britain then guaranteed only Germany's western borders at Locarno in 1925. When Germany used the settlement's own principle of self-determination to claim the Rhineland, Austria and the Sudetenland, Britain and France judged the claims fair or the risk too high, and did not act."

**What it set up.** Too Germany-centred. Three of the four items are German, and the Ruhr (1923) is the least consequential of them. For a world reader:
- keep **May Fourth Movement (1919)**;
- keep **Remilitarisation of the Rhineland (1936)**, the cleanest test of the enforcement thesis;
- replace the Ruhr with **March on Rome (1922)**, Italy's "mutilated victory" becoming fascism;
- replace the Anschluss with **Munich Agreement (1938)**, where self-determination was turned against the settlement with British and French consent. If a Middle East slot is wanted instead, use **Arab Revolt in Palestine (1936–1939)** or **Great Syrian Revolt (1925–1927)**.

**Today.** The Trianon line is accurate but European and niche. A world reader would get more from a mandate legacy, such as the Iraq–Syria and Jordan borders or Lebanon's confessional state, both rooted in the mandate period. Optional; the editor's call.

**The detail.** Strong. Keep it. It also quietly supports the counter-case, since the victors refused to apply the principle of equality to themselves.

## Better bottom line (50 words)
> The Paris settlement broke up the defeated empires into nation-states and mandates on a self-determination principle it applied selectively, so European revisionists and colonial nationalists rejected it from the start. When Britain and France declined to enforce it in the 1930s, Germany had overturned its European terms by 1939.

If the editor wants to keep the draft's enforcement thesis central and Germany-led, here is a fallback (44 words):
> The Paris settlement left Germany intact among weak new states and wrote terms that only Britain and France would enforce once the US Senate refused the treaty. Their choice not to act in the 1930s let Germany dismantle the European order by 1939.

## Flags for the fact-checker (new claims introduced above)
Maysalun (July 1920); Fiume and the "mutilated victory"; March on Rome (October 1922); Rapallo (April 1922); Locarno (1925) guaranteeing western borders only; the minorities treaties; roughly 3 million Germans in Czechoslovakia; Great Syrian Revolt (1925–27); Arab Revolt in Palestine (1936–39); the armistice preceding any invasion of Germany.
