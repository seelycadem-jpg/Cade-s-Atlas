# The Paris Peace Settlement, 1919–1923

## Bottom line
The victors tried to contain Germany and remake Europe and the Middle East as nation-states, and wrote a peace harsh enough to breed revision that lost its American guarantee when the US Senate refused to ratify. By 1938 Germany had remilitarised the Rhineland and annexed Austria.

## Why it happened
The Central Powers' defeat left the victors, with no German or Soviet seat, to partition the Habsburg and Ottoman empires and disarm Germany. French Prime Minister Georges Clemenceau wanted Germany weakened; US President Woodrow Wilson wanted a League of Nations, which Britain and France conceded.

## What it did
- Versailles (1919): Germany lost 13% of its European territory, a tenth of its people and its colonies; army capped at 100,000.
- Reparations (1921): 132 billion gold marks, 50 billion meant to be paid; Germany paid 20.6 billion by 1932.
- Trianon (1920): Hungary kept 28% of its land and 36% of its people.
- Lausanne (1923) replaced Sèvres after Turkish nationalists overturned it.
- Mandates: Palestine, Transjordan and Iraq to Britain; Syria and Lebanon to France.

## Winners and losers
France regained Alsace-Lorraine; Japan took Germany's Shandong rights. Hungary lost proportionally most, leaving 3.3 million Hungarians abroad. Some 1.6 million Greek Orthodox and Muslims paid in Lausanne's compulsory exchange.

## Why it held or failed
The settlement needed an enforcer it never had. France traded its claim to a Rhine frontier for an Anglo-American guarantee, which lapsed when the Senate's 49–35 vote in March 1920 fell short of two-thirds. Germany kept 87% of its European territory, so the terms held only while Britain and France enforced them. The victors wrote terms needing permanent enforcement and relied on a Senate that refused.

## What it set up
- May Fourth Movement (1919)
- Occupation of the Ruhr (1923)
- Remilitarisation of the Rhineland (1936)
- Anschluss (1938)

## Today
Hungary marks 4 June, the Trianon signing date, as the Day of National Cohesion.

## The detail
Japan's racial equality clause for the League Covenant won 11 of 17 votes in commission on 11 April 1919; Wilson, in the chair, ruled it needed unanimity, and it failed.
