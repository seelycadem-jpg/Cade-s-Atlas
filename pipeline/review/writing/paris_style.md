# Style review: The Paris Peace Settlement, 1919–1923 (the Haass/Bremmer desk)

Scope: writing only. Facts and judgments are left as the draft has them. Sources section ignored.

## 1. Violations by section

**Overall**
- Length: 298 words for sections 2–8, inside the 230–300 band but at the ceiling. The words go to restatement (the failure argument is made twice) and leave gaps elsewhere (winners and losers has no verdict words).
- Judgment density is uneven. Bottom line sentence 2, What it did, Winners and losers and the Lausanne bullet describe without judging.
- The page promises Europe *and* the Middle East in the bottom line, but every judgment after that is about Germany. The edit cannot fix this without new substance, so it goes to the red team and the merge.

**Bottom line**
- Sentence 1 is overloaded: purpose, then outcome, then cause of failure in one 36-word chain. "harsh enough to breed revision that lost its American guarantee" lets "that lost" attach to "revision", so it reads as if the revision lost the guarantee.
- Sentence 1 gives purpose and outcome but never states the judgment on the order: the peace depended on enforcement it did not have.
- Sentence 2 lists two events. It gives no consequence for the order, so a reader who stops here learns that things happened in 1936–38 without learning what they meant. It fails the "a reader could quote it" test.

**Why it happened**
- Sentence 1 holds a parenthetical ("with no German or Soviet seat") in the middle of its main clause, and that breaks the line.
- The forces are thin. "The Central Powers' defeat" is a premise, not a force, and the sentence describes the task without judging it.
- Sentence 2 is symmetrical rhythm ("Clemenceau wanted X; Wilson wanted Y"), and its tail ("which Britain and France conceded") is limp. The concession is the real decision, so it should carry the sentence.

**What it did**
- The frames are mixed. Germany "lost 13%" and Hungary "kept 28%", which hides the one comparison that matters. The 87% figure appears only later, in Why it failed. Use "kept" for both.
- "50 billion meant to be paid" is vague insider shorthand. Say what it means: the payable portion.
- The Lausanne bullet has no number and no term. It reports a sequence ("replaced Sèvres after nationalists overturned it") and never says what either treaty did.
- Saint-Germain and Neuilly appear only as strands. This is acceptable under the five-bullet cap, but noted.

**Winners and losers**
- No sentence names a winner or a loser as such. The section lists transfers and leaves the reader to sort them, which is description rather than judgment.
- China, the other side of Japan's Shandong gain, is missing as a loser. Germany's losses appear only in What it did, which is acceptable. Under "paid", the exchange victims are the only entry.
- "lost proportionally most" is clumsy, and "leaving 3.3 million Hungarians abroad" is a loose participle that trails the sentence.

**Why it held or failed**
- The first sentence is the strongest line on the page. Keep it.
- Sentence 2 is overloaded: the trade, the lapse, the vote, the tally and the threshold all sit in one sentence, and the misjudgment gets lost.
- The mechanism is out of order. The 87% fact (why enforcement was needed) comes after the guarantee (who would enforce), so the reader gets the cure before the disease.
- Sentence 4 restates sentence 1 ("needed an enforcer it never had" / "terms needing permanent enforcement"), and as a closer it reads as an aphorism. No misjudgment is attributed in plain words: who bet on what.
- For the fact-checker, not changed here: the 49–35 vote in March 1920 was on Versailles, not on the guarantee treaty, which lapsed as a result. The sentence currently implies the vote was on the guarantee.

**What it set up**
- These are bare names. They are compliant ("each the name of a later event"). A clause per item would make each causal link visible, but the page has no word budget left for that (see the notes on the edit).
- No Middle East or Turkish consequence appears, although the bottom line claims the Middle East. This is a red-team and merge point.

**Today**
- Compliant. The line is checkable and live.

**The detail**
- "11 of 17 votes ... on 11 April" puts two 11s together and makes the reader stop. "and it failed" is a limp ending. Lead with the date, then the result.

**Strands**
- Compliant.

**Banned list**
- No banned words, no negation-then-reveal, no storytelling opening, no named historians. Two items are close: the symmetrical semicolon pair in Why it happened, and the restating closer in Why it held or failed, which functions as an aphorism.

## 2. Line edit

# The Paris Peace Settlement, 1919–1923

## Bottom line
The victors set out to contain Germany and remake Europe and the Middle East as nation-states, and wrote a peace harsh enough to invite revision but enforceable only with an American guarantee the US Senate let lapse. Britain and France enforced it until 1936; by 1938 Germany had broken it.

## Why it happened
Defeat of the Central Powers let the victors, excluding Germany and Soviet Russia, disarm Germany and partition the Habsburg and Ottoman empires. French Prime Minister Georges Clemenceau wanted Germany weakened. Britain and France accepted US President Woodrow Wilson's League of Nations to keep him on side.

## What it did
- Versailles (1919): Germany kept 87% of its European territory, lost a tenth of its people and its colonies; army capped at 100,000.
- Reparations (1921): 132 billion gold marks nominal, 50 billion payable; 20.6 billion paid by 1932.
- Trianon (1920): Hungary kept 28% of its land and 36% of its people.
- Lausanne (1923) fixed Turkey's borders after Turkish nationalists overturned Sèvres.
- Mandates: Palestine, Transjordan, Iraq to Britain; Syria, Lebanon to France.

## Winners and losers
France regained Alsace-Lorraine; Britain and France took the Arab mandates. Japan took Germany's Shandong rights at China's expense. Hungary lost most: 3.3 million Hungarians ended up abroad. Some 1.6 million Greek Orthodox and Muslims paid in Lausanne's compulsory exchange.

## Why it held or failed
The settlement needed an enforcer it never had. Germany emerged largely intact, so the terms held only while Britain and France enforced them. The victors staked that enforcement on the US Senate: France dropped its Rhine frontier claim for an Anglo-American guarantee, which lapsed when the Senate's 49–35 vote in March 1920 fell short of two-thirds.

## What it set up
- May Fourth Movement (1919)
- Occupation of the Ruhr (1923)
- Remilitarisation of the Rhineland (1936)
- Anschluss (1938)

## Today
Hungary marks 4 June, the Trianon signing date, as the Day of National Cohesion.

## The detail
On 11 April 1919 Japan's racial equality clause for the League Covenant won 11 of 17 votes in commission. Wilson, chairing, ruled that it needed unanimity, and the clause died.

## Strands
- Paris Peace Conference (1919–1920)
- Treaty of Versailles (1919)
- Treaty of Saint-Germain (1919)
- Treaty of Neuilly (1919)
- Treaty of Trianon (1920)
- Treaty of Sèvres (1920)
- League of Nations and the mandates (1920)
- Treaty of Lausanne (1923)

**Notes on the edit**
- The bottom line is 50 words, at the limit. Sentence 1 gives purpose, outcome and the fault line. Sentence 2 gives the consequence for the order. The quotable part is "harsh enough to invite revision but enforceable only with an American guarantee the US Senate let lapse."
- "nominal" / "payable" glosses the A/B versus C bond split already in the draft's sources. No new figure is added.
- "at China's expense" and "Britain and France took the Arab mandates" put verdict words on facts the draft already states (Shandong, mandates). The merge should confirm this does not count as new substance.
- In Why it held or failed, the restating closer is cut. Its judgment (the victors relied on a Senate that refused) moves into sentence 3 as the named misjudgment, ahead of the vote.
- Sections 2–8 run to 300 words (draft 298), exactly at the ceiling. That leaves no room for one-clause glosses under What it set up. If the merge wants them, they must be paid for elsewhere: the Trianon bullet overlaps the Hungary line in Winners and losers and is the first cut.

## 3. Scores ("would this run in Foreign Affairs or a Eurasia Group note")

- **Draft: 6/10.** The facts are hard and the core judgment ("needed an enforcer it never had") is right. The bottom line buries that judgment in an overloaded sentence and ends on a list of events, and two sections describe rather than judge.
- **Edit: 8/10.** The bottom line now leads with the fault line and the consequence, and every section carries a verdict. It still reads as a Germany page under a Europe-and-Middle-East heading, and only the red team and the merge can fix that.
